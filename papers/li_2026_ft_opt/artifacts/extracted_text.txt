Abstract: Overtone photothermal (OPT) microscopy is a promising modality for vibrational volumetric imaging in 8 
aqueous samples, owing to reduced water absorption and increased penetration depth. However, the wavelength-9 
scanned, frequency-domain implementations impose a trade-off among spectral bandwidth, acquisition speed, and 10 
spectral resolution, limiting rapid and information-rich chemical fingerprinting. Here, we introduce Fourier-transform 11 
overtone photothermal (FT-OPT) spectroscopy and microscopy, a time-domain framework that retrieves photothermal 12 
spectra without sequential wavelength tuning. In FT-OPT, interferometric modulation of a supercontinuum pump 13 
excites broadband overtone transitions, and the resulting local thermal lens simultaneously encodes the vibrations into 14 
a temporal interferogram. This signal is sensitively read out by a shorter-wavelength probe beam, thereby preserving 15

1 Introduction 26 
Vibrational spectroscopy and imaging1±4, revealing intrinsic signatures of molecular structure, are 27 
indispensable tools for label-free and chemically specific detection across environmental science, 28 
biology, medicine, chemistry, and materials science. In particular, owing to its ultrahigh sensitivity, 29 
enhanced spatial resolution and optical sectioning capability, mid-infrared (MIR) photothermal 30 
microscopy5 has attracted increasing attention over the past decade6±10. By using a shortwave beam 31 
to detect the local thermal lens effect11 induced by MIR absorption under high numerical aperture 32 
(NA) conditions, this approach achieves micromolar sensitivity and submicron spatial resolution. 33 
Nevertheless, MIR-based methods require tunable and broadband sources such as quantum 34 
cascade lasers (QCLs), optical parametric oscillators (OPOs), or synchrotron radiation 12, as well 35 
as other specialized optical materials and components, which substantially increase system 36 
complexity and cost. More critically, the strong absorption of water leads to severe signal 37 
background and attenuation, often necessitating measurements under dry or dehydrated 38 
conditions13±15. This effect constrains applications in aqueous environments and restricts 39 
penetration depth in complex biological tissues 5. Recently, researchers proposed overtone 40 
photothermal (OPT) microscopy16,17 based on shortwave infrared (SWIR, 1000±×QP While 41 
preserving the advantages of photothermal microscopy, exciting overtone transitions with a SWIR 42 
source dramatically suppresses water absorption 17, which not only reduces system cost but also 43 
enables 3D imaging of biological samples at millimeter depths16. 44 
Although demonstrating outstanding imaging ability, photothermal detection cannot obtain spectra 45 
via a normal spectrometer; instead, spectra are acquired by tuning the pump wavelength. This 46 
frequency-domain excitation approach introduces a series of constraints in spectral performance 47 
due to the limitations of mainstream tunable SWIR sources3,18. On the one hand, the tuning range 48 
is difficult to cover several hundred nanometers or more 19. As a result, existing OPT microscopy 49 
typically covers only a portion of the C ±H overtone region17. Due to the notable spectral overlap 50 
of overtone and combination transitions, a broader spectral range is required to extract more 51 
chemically specific information20. On the other hand, prevailing SWIR sources (e.g., OPOs) also 52 
suffer from limited tuning speeds: manual adjustment typically requires a few seconds17, and even 53 
most automated tunable sources operate on timescales of tens to hundreds of milliseconds per 54 
wavelength step. Owing to the positive coupling between acquisition time and spectral bandwidth, 55 
it is necessary to spend several seconds or minutes to acquire a spectrum, which is unsuitable for 56

dynamic spectral applications. Additionally, because most of these sources operate as ultrafast 57 
pulses19, their short durations fundamentally lead to spectral broadening due to the time ±energy 58 
uncertainty principle17. While the broad linewidths in overtone regions reduce the requirement for 59 
spectral resolution, high spectral resolution remains advantageous for distinguishing similar 60 
molecules. 61 
Inspired by FTIR and dual-comb spectroscopy 21, time-domain excitation schemes have recently 62 
been explored in photothermal microscopy 12,22 as a tentative approach to overcome the above 63 
trade-offs, but most remain at the proof-of-concept stage. Even in strong fundamental MIR 64 
absorption bands, their photothermal signals remain weak, hindering label-free detection. 65 
Therefore, existing demonstrations require thermosensitive fluorescent labeling and long 66 
integration (~10 min per spectrum). This performance not only cannot accommodate dynamic 67 
spectral monitoring or hyperspectral imaging, but also preserve the above limitations of MIR-68 
based approaches. This may critically underestimate the potential of time-domain excitation in 69 
photothermal spectroscopy and microscopy. 70 
To address these challenges, in this work, we present Fourier-transform overtone photothermal 71 
(FT-OPT) spectroscopy and microscopy, which replaces the conventional frequency-domain 72 
excitation with a time-domain interference strategy. To realize this concept, we employed a 73 
femtosecond laser and photonic crystal fiber (PCF) to generate a high-power supercontinuum as 74 
SWIR pump, enabling intense photothermal signals without the need for labeling or long 75 
integration times. By encoding and Fourier-transform decoding the local thermal lens dynamic, 76 
FT-OPT decouples the trade-off between spectral bandwidth and acquisition time, obtaining 77 
spectra with ultimate spectral resolution within hundreds of milliseconds. The parallel and 78 
photothermal measurement23 also endows it with high sensitivity. The supercontinuum bandwidth 79 
encompasses the C ±H 2nd and O ±H 1st overtone regions, enabling clear discrimination and 80 
unmixing of C8 hydrocarbons, even in overtone regions with severe spectral overlap. We further 81 
develop the first microfluidic overtone spectroscopy for dynamic spectral monitoring. Moreover, 82 
we demonstrate hyperspectral (4D) photothermal imaging for the first time within a time-domain 83 
excitation framework. 84

A major advantage of photothermal imaging is that it operates as a pump ±probe ³two-photon´ 123 
process, inherently providing axial optical sectioning capability 5,17. Moreover, the lateral point 124 
spread function (PSF) is jointly determined by the pump and probe beams, allowing the use of a 125 
shorter-wavelength probe to further enhance spatial resolution. 4D hyperspectral images can be 126 
obtained by performing point-by-point scanning in the X±Y-Z volume based on the above pump-127 
probe processes. Another advantage is that, particularly in overtone applications, photothermal 128 
microscopy offers a significant sensitivity advantage over Beer ±Lambert±based methods. This is 129 
because overtone transitions are several orders of magnitude weaker than fundamental transitions, 130 
and the light±matter interaction length is extremely short (several micrometers), making the pump 131 
intensity modulation depth inherently limited. Photothermal detection improves sensitivity by 132 
converting weak absorption into a measurable thermal response, while time-domain excitation 133 
introduces a multiplex (Fellgett) advantage23 that further enhances the signal. Taking lipids as an 134 
example, the present FT-OPT system achieves ~86-fold enhancement in signal modulation depth 135 
(0.075% vs 6.5%). A detailed analysis is provided in the Supplementary Information and Fig. S1. 136

modulated by a chopper at fm=10kHz with a 50% duty cycle, and the relative delay 2 was 163

XY±Z scanning was performed using motorized translation stages at the sample and objective to 165

2.4 FT-OPT for molecular identification and unmixing 240 
In addition to weak signal intensity, broad linewidths and severe spectral overlap in the overtone 241

sophisticated algorithms to improve molecular discrimination 34, but concerns remain about their 243

2.5 Microfluidic dynamic spectral monitoring of FT-OPT 285 
Although microfluidic photothermal detection for liquids or particles has been demonstrated 35, it 286 
has so far been restricted to single-wavelength or spectrally discrete multi-wavelength operation. 287 
To date, the integration with continuous broadband photothermal spectroscopy or overtone 288 
spectroscopy remains unrealized. This is because the two spectroscopic techniques each impose 289 
stringent requirements: rapid spectral acquisition or high sensitivity, respectively. FT-OPT enables 290 
sensitive overtone spectroscopy within a few hundred milliseconds, thereby supporting dynamic 291 
spectral monitoring. This provides a foundation for label-free spectral flow cytometry 36, which is 292 
challenging to achieve with MIR approaches due to strong water absorption in aqueous 293 
environments of cytometers. To verify this, we constructed a simple microfluidic system and used 294 
a solenoid three-way valve to alternately switch water and cyclooctane through the focal volume 295 
for dynamic FT-OPT tests. As shown in Fig. 4(a), water and cyclooctane form a distinct crescent-296 
shaped two-phase interface within the outer tubing and microchannel. 297 
Intuitively, the flow velocity affects the modulation depth of the photothermal signal. An 298 
excessively high velocity rapidly removes heat from the focal region, thereby limiting the 299 
maximum temperature rise and accelerating thermal dissipation. To evaluate this effect, we 300 
measured the photothermal responses under different flow velocities. Without relative delay 301 
scanning, the APD signals were processed using match filtering 37 and normalized to a maximum 302 
value of 3 V. The results are shown in Fig. 4(d,e), corresponding to water and cyclooctane, 303 
respectively. This effect becomes pronounced when the velocity exceeds the order of hundreds of 304 
millimeters per second. The thermal lens response exhibits a tendency toward saturation, with both 305 
faster heating and accelerated thermal relaxation. Fig. 3(f) shows a decreasing photothermal 306 
modulation depth for both substances with increasing flow velocity, further supporting this 307 
conclusion. Since a flow velocity of 100 mm/s is already higher than that required for most 308 
microfluidic applications, a velocity of 30 mm/s was adopted in the following experiments. 309 
The spectral refresh period was set to ~0.842 s, yielding an effective duty cycle of ~51%, as the 310 
relative delay scanning and stage turnaround each occupied nearly half of a cycle. The refresh rate 311 
is currently limited by the stage reversal time and the optical chopper modulation frequency, the 312 
latter constraining the maximum allowable relative delay scanning speed according to the Nyquist 313 
sampling theorem. These limitations arise from the present hardware implementation rather than 314 
the fundamental FT-OPT mechanism. Fig. 4(c) presents the spectral monitoring results acquired 315
